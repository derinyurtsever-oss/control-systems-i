Lecture 3 · 30 September

State-space models, linearization

✨AI-generated recap — check anything important against the slides

Almost nothing you will meet is linear. This lecture is about why that matters far less than it sounds — as long as you pick the right point and stay close to it.

Four questions to ask of any system

Linear?

Do sums and scalings go straight through? Then superposition works: split the input, solve each piece, add.

Causal?

Does the output need the future? Anything you can actually build is causal.

Static?

Is the present input enough? If the system remembers anything — any ODE — it is dynamic.

Time-invariant?

Does it matter when you apply the input? Shift the input, and the output just shifts with it.

The course works only with systems that pass all four (and have one input, one output): LTI SISO. Very restrictive — and yet most real controllers are designed exactly this way.

A trap worth remembering

  • ẋ = 1 + u looks linear, and is not. An input of 1 gives y = 2t; an input of 2 gives 3t, not 4t. The constant breaks homogeneity.
  • The fix is the whole idea of the lecture in miniature: hold u at −1, where the system sits still, and work in deviations from there. Then δẋ = δu, which is perfectly linear.

State-space, formally

  • The state at t₀, together with the input from then on, is enough to predict the output for all later times. It is the system's memory.
  • Its dimension is the system's order. A memoryless system has a zero-dimensional state; a pure time delay needs an infinite one.
  • LTI systems all fit one form: ẋ = Ax + Bu, y = Cx + Du. If D = 0, the system is strictly causal.

Linearization in three moves

  • Find an equilibrium: an (xe, ue) where f(xe, ue) = 0. Start there, hold the input there, and nothing moves.
  • Take the Taylor expansion around it and throw away everything past first order.
  • Read off the Jacobians: A = ∂f/∂x, B = ∂f/∂u, C = ∂g/∂x, D = ∂g/∂u, all evaluated at the equilibrium.
  • The x, u and y in the result are deviations from the equilibrium, not the real quantities — the δ's usually get dropped, and that is where mistakes come from.

The pendulum has two answers

Hanging down · (0, 0)

A = [0 1; −g/l −c/ml²]. The stable equilibrium.

Balanced up · (π, 0)

A = [0 1; +g/l −c/ml²]. The unstable one.

Same system, one sign different, completely different behaviour. Which point you linearize around is the first decision, not a detail.

How near is near?

  • For the pendulum the only approximation is sin θ ≈ θ, and its error grows like θ²/6: 0.1% at 5°, 1.2% at 15°, 4.7% at 30°.
  • So a swing kept inside ±15° obeys the linear model to about a percent — better than you know the friction, and far better than you know the child. Past 30° the linear model starts telling stories.
  • What was thrown away is second order, so halving the excursion quarters the error.

If you keep one thing

Near an equilibrium the linear model is not a compromise you tolerate — it is the right picture. If the linearization is stable, so is the real system near that point (Lyapunov). That is the licence for everything else in the course. Just never forget which point, and how far you have strayed.

  • Release the two pendulums at 5°, 30° and 90°, then linearize at the top — and watch the linear model fall apart in both directions.
