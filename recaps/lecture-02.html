Modeling

✨AI-generated recap — check anything important against the slides

Last week the plant was a box with a letter on it. This week you open the box, and it turns out almost everything inside is one of three shapes.

Step 1 — draw the boundary

  • Inputs split in two: the ones you manipulate (endogenous, the control u) and the ones the world manipulates (exogenous — disturbances, d).
  • Outputs split too: what you can actually measure, and what you actually care about. They are often not the same signal.
  • State is the system's memory — the summary of every past input. Parameters are what never changes.
  • A check worth keeping: if one system's output feeds another, it must not disturb the first — unless you meant to build a loop.

Step 2 — the bathtub law

Nearly every model in the lecture came from one sentence: watch a stored quantity, and count what flows in and out.

  • d(storage)/dt = Σ inflows − Σ outflows.
  • It does not care what is being stored: water, heat, momentum, or money.
  • Keep differentiating the output until the input shows up explicitly.

Step 3 — standard form

  • Everything gets written as ẋ = f(x, u), y = h(x, u) — first derivatives only.
  • A higher-order equation becomes a stack of first-order ones: name each derivative as its own state. A swing is two states; a horizontal quadrotor is four.

Six examples, three shapes

Integrator

The water tank. Flow in minus flow out, and the level just accumulates.

First-order lag

A heated room, a car's speed. It heads towards a value and eases off as it gets there.

Oscillator

The swing. Energy sloshes between two stores, so it rings rather than settles.

A vertical quadrotor is two integrators; a horizontal one is those shapes wired together. This is why a whole semester on a handful of transfer functions is not a toy exercise.

Reading the time scale before solving anything

  • τ ≈ storage ÷ flow, straight off the equations.
  • A well-insulated room: τ = m/c, hours. Open a window and τ collapses.
  • A swing has nothing to dissipate, so the number that matters is ωn = √(g/l) — about 2 rad/s on a 2.5 m rope, a three-second period, exactly what a playground looks like.
  • A car left alone coasts down over about a minute; pushed by the engine it is more like ten seconds. How fast a plant drifts and how hard the actuator can push are different questions, and it is usually the second that decides what a controller can do.

Wiring systems together

  • Series Σ₂Σ₁ · parallel Σ₁ + Σ₂ · feedback (I + Σ₁Σ₂)⁻¹Σ₁.
  • For a simple loop with gain K, y = K/(1 + K)·u. Watch what that does as K approaches −1: the output runs away for any input at all.
  • Three architectures: feed-forward, feedback, and two degrees of freedom — feed-forward for tracking, feedback for everything that goes wrong.

If you keep one thing

All models are wrong, and feedback is what lets a wrong one be good enough. A factor-of-four error in the plant gain came out as half a percent at the output, because 1/(1 + kK) was small. That is the licence to model simply — not an excuse to model carelessly.

Worth doing this week

  • Read Åström & Murray, chapter 3 — the suggested reading for this lecture.
  • Play with three pasts, one state: three cups with completely different mornings, all holding 200 ml, all with the same future.
  • Take one system from the worksheet and write it in standard form without looking — boundary, bathtub law, first-order stack.
