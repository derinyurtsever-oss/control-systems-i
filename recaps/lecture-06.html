Poles and zeros

✨AI-generated recap — check anything important against the slides

Last week gave us G(s). This lecture is about reading it: factor the top and the bottom, mark the roots in the complex plane, and almost everything about the response can be seen from where those points sit.

One transfer function, four ways to write it

Polynomials

N(s)/D(s) + d — what you get straight from C(sI − A)−1B + D.

Partial fractions

Σ ri/(s − pi) + r0. One term per pole; the residues ri say how much each one contributes.

Root-locus form

k·Π(s − zi)/Π(s − pi). Best for computing G by hand, and for the root locus next week.

Bode form

Factors written as (s/(−p) + 1). Used for Bode plots in a few weeks.

  • The poles pi are the roots of the denominator, which are the eigenvalues of A. The zeros zi are the roots of the numerator.
  • In MATLAB: ss and tf build the system, residue gives partial fractions, zpk gives the factored form.

Computing G(s) with arrows

  • Every factor (s − p) is the arrow from p to s in the complex plane. Multiplying complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles.
  • So |G(s)| = k · (product of distances from the zeros) / (product of distances from the poles).
  • And ∠G(s) = (sum of angles from the zeros) − (sum of angles from the poles).
  • The worked example: G(s) = 2(s + 1)/(s³ + 4s² + 6s + 4) has poles at −2 and −1 ± j. At s = j the arrows give |G(j)| = 2√2/5 ≈ 0.57 and ∠G(j) = −45°, so sin(t) comes out as about 0.57·sin(t − 45°).
  • Rule of thumb: s close to a pole makes |G| large, and s close to a zero makes it small.

Test inputs

  • Step: u = 1 for t ≥ 0. For a stable system it ends at G(0) — 0.5 in the example above.
  • Impulse: one hammer blow with area 1. Its response is CeAtB, the same as starting from x(0) = B. It is G(s) itself in disguise: the impulse response determines the system completely.
  • The step response is the running area under the impulse response.

Poles make the shapes

  • Write G in partial fractions and the impulse response is Σ riepit: each pole contributes one mode.
  • A real pole gives an exponential. A complex pair gives an oscillation inside the envelope r·eσt. The further left the pole, the faster it fades; the higher up, the faster it oscillates.
  • Find the residues with the cover-up method: hide the factor (s − pi) and evaluate what is left at s = pi.

Zeros change the residues

  • Zeros never change which modes exist — those come from the poles. They change how much of each mode you see.
  • A zero right next to a pole makes that residue tiny, so the mode is muffled.
  • If a zero lands exactly on a pole, they cancel: the mode disappears from G, but not from the system. That is fine for a stable pole and dangerous for an unstable one. Never cancel an unstable pole.

Zeros borrow the derivative

  • Multiplying by s means taking the derivative. So adding a zero, G = (s/(−z) + 1)·G̃, gives y = ỹ + (1/(−z))·dỹ/dt.
  • A left half-plane zero adds a positive derivative: the response is earlier, faster, and tends to overshoot.
  • A right half-plane zero is called non-minimum phase. It adds a negative derivative, so the output first moves the wrong way. Stability does not change, but it makes control much harder; often the fix is to measure a different output.

If you keep one thing

The poles decide which shapes the response is built from. The zeros decide how much of each shape you actually get. With the arrows from the poles and zeros you can read |G| and ∠G straight off the plane.
