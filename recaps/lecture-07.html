Feedback and the root locus

✨AI-generated recap — check anything important against the slides

Close the loop with a gain k and the poles move. Doing the algebra again for every k is tedious and tells you little. The root locus is a quick sketch of where the closed-loop poles go as k runs from 0 to ∞, drawn from nothing but the open-loop poles and zeros.

Watching the poles move

  • With k in front of 1/(s + 1), the closed loop is k/(s + 1 + k), so the single pole sits at −1 − k. Turning up k pushes it further left, which makes the system faster.
  • With (s + 2)/(s² + 2s + 2) the poles start at −1 ± j. As k grows they swing round, meet on the real axis, and then one heads to the zero at −2 while the other runs off to −∞.
  • In general, with kL(s) = kN(s)/D(s), the closed loop is T = kL/(1 + kL) and its poles are the roots of D(s) + kN(s) = 0.

The rules for sketching it

  • Symmetric about the real axis: poles are real or come in conjugate pairs.
  • There are as many closed-loop poles as open-loop poles.
  • They start at the open-loop poles (k → 0) and end at the open-loop zeros (k → ∞). Any poles left over head off to infinity.
  • Angle rule: s is on the locus when the angles from the zeros minus the angles from the poles add up to 180° (for k > 0). This is the rule the others come from.
  • Magnitude rule: once s is on the locus, |N(s)|/|D(s)| = 1/|k| tells you which k puts the pole there.

Shortcuts that come out of the angle rule

  • On the real axis: a point is on the positive-k locus if there is an odd number of real poles and zeros to its right. Where two branches meet on the axis they break away from it or break back in.
  • Asymptotes: the n − m poles that go to infinity leave at angles of 180°/(n − m) plus multiples of 360°/(n − m). So two excess poles go straight up and down, and three leave at ±60° and 180°.
  • The asymptotes meet on the real axis at the "centre of mass": (Σ poles − Σ zeros)/(n − m), with poles counting as positive masses and zeros as negative ones.

The inverted pendulum, three ways

P control

P(s) = 1/(s² − g/l) has a pole on each side of zero. A big enough k pulls them onto the imaginary axis, but no further. It works like attaching a spring: it swings forever.

PI control

Adds a pole at 0 and a zero. The two asymptotes then sit in the right half-plane, so it still does not help.

PD control

The zero from the derivative term attracts the branches into the left half-plane, and the pendulum can be stabilized.

Realizable PD

A pure s cannot be built, so the D term becomes s/(s/100 + 1). That adds a far-away pole at −100 and barely changes the picture.

Dynamic compensators and the names that come with them

  • A plain gain is often not enough, so the controller itself becomes a transfer function C(s): PI, PD, PID, and lead (zero closer to the origin than the pole) or lag (pole closer than the zero).
  • The loop gain is L(s) = P(s)C(s). Its poles and zeros are those of the plant plus those of the controller.
  • From r to y: the complementary sensitivity T = kL/(1 + kL). From r to e: the sensitivity S = 1/(1 + kL).

Two traps

  • Well-posedness: if both blocks pass the input straight through (non-zero D), the loop can turn into 1 + D1D2 = 0, which has no meaning. Avoid direct feedthrough in feedback loops.
  • Internal stability: with C = 1/(s − 1) and P = (s − 1)/(s + 1), the r → y transfer function 1/(s + 2) looks fine — but r → u is unstable, so the controller is blowing up inside. Every transfer function between any two signals in the loop has to be stable. Never cancel an unstable pole with a zero.

If you keep one thing

Closed-loop poles start at the open-loop poles and end at the zeros or at infinity. Poles push the branches away and zeros pull them in. A rough sketch is usually enough to see whether a controller can work at all.
