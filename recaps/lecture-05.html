Transfer functions

✨AI-generated recap — check anything important against the slides

Last week the initial-condition response became easy: just read the eigenvalues. The forced response was still a convolution integral you had to grind through for every new input. This lecture makes it just as easy, using one idea: there is a kind of input that every LTI system simply hands back, multiplied by a number.

The trick: exponential inputs

  • Feed in u(t) = est, where s = σ + jω is a complex number. Real s gives a growing or decaying exponential, imaginary s gives "half" a sinusoid, and anything in between gives a sinusoid inside an exponential envelope.
  • Work the integral through and the output splits cleanly in two: CeAt[x(0) − (sI − A)−1B], the transient, plus [C(sI − A)−1B + D] est, the steady state.
  • If the system is asymptotically stable the transient dies out, and what is left is just the input times a number.

The transfer function

  • That number is G(s) = C(sI − A)−1B + D: in steady state the input est comes out as G(s)·est.
  • For a finite-dimensional system it is always a proper ratio of two polynomials in s: the numerator degree is never higher than the denominator degree.
  • Two examples: ẋ = −x + u gives 1/(s + 1). The mass-spring-damper with m = k = c = 1 gives 1/(s² + s + 1).
  • An integrator is 1/s and a differentiator is s. The differentiator is not proper, so no state-space model realizes it exactly; in practice you use s/(εs + 1) instead.

Sinusoid in, sinusoid out

  • Write cos(ωt) = ½ejωt + ½e−jωt and push each half through G separately.
  • In steady state the output is |G(jω)| · cos(ωt + ∠G(jω)): the same frequency, stretched by |G(jω)| and shifted by ∠G(jω).
  • For 1/(s + 1) at ω = 2π, the output is about 0.16 times as large and 81° behind.

Poles, and what happens at them

  • The denominator of G(s) is the characteristic polynomial of A, so the poles are the eigenvalues — unless a factor cancels, which comes up next week.
  • If you feed in est with s exactly at a pole, G(s) blows up and the response gains a t·est term.
  • A pole at 0 driven by a constant grows like t. A pole at ±jω driven by cos(ωt) gives an oscillation that keeps growing: resonance. A bounded input then gives an unbounded output, so the system is not BIBO stable.

Any input is a sum of exponentials

  • Think of signals as vectors with a scalar product. The exponentials est then form a basis: for periodic signals this is the Fourier series.
  • The weights of that basis are the Laplace transform, U(s) = ∫ u(t)e−st dt. Analysis III teaches you how to compute it; here you only need the idea.
  • By superposition every weight just gets multiplied by G(s): Y(s) = G(s)·U(s). This is the complete output, transient included.

Going back and forth

In series

G2(s)·G1(s): you just multiply.

In parallel

G1(s) + G2(s): you just add.

In feedback

G1 / (1 + G1G2), with negative feedback.

Back to state space

Use partial fractions for a diagonal A, or the controllable canonical form: the denominator coefficients go in the last row of A, the numerator coefficients into C.

  • Why bother going back: you design a controller C(s) as a transfer function, but a microcontroller needs state space. Euler steps x ← x + (Ax + Be)dt and u = Cx + De, run forever.

If you keep one thing

Exponentials pass through any LTI system unchanged in shape, only multiplied by G(s). For a sinusoid that means the same frequency comes out, stretched by |G(jω)| and delayed by ∠G(jω). Calculus becomes algebra.

Worth doing this week

  • Read Åström & Murray, sections 8.1–8.3 and 8.5; skip time delays and PDEs.
  • Compute G(s) for the mass-spring-damper yourself with the 2×2 inverse formula, then check that the poles are the eigenvalues of A.
  • Pick an ω, work out |G(jω)| and ∠G(jω) by hand, and sketch the steady-state output on top of the input.
