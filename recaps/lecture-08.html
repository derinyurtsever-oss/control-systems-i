Specifications and PID

✨AI-generated recap — check anything important against the slides

The root locus tells you whether a loop is stable. This lecture asks how well it behaves: how fast, how much overshoot, how close to the target in the end. Each answer turns into a region of the complex plane where the closed-loop poles have to be. P, I and D are the tools for getting them there.

First-order step response

  • G(s) = 1/(τs + 1) gives y(t) = 1 − e−t/τ. It ends at G(0) = 1.
  • After one τ it is 63% of the way there. The pole sits at −1/τ.
  • Settling time to within d%: Td = τ·ln(100/d). For 5% that is about 3τ.

Second-order step response

  • G(s) = ωn²/(s² + 2ζωns + ωn²). When underdamped (ζ < 1) the poles are σ ± jω.
  • Reading the pole: ωn is its distance from the origin, |σ| = ζωn, and ζ is the sine of its angle away from the imaginary axis.
  • The envelope decays like eσt, so settling works as in first order with τ = 1/|σ|.
  • Time to peak: Tp = π/ω. Overshoot: Mp = eσπ/ω, which depends only on ζ.
  • Three numbers worth remembering: ζ = 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 give about 37%, 16% and 5% overshoot.

Dominant poles

  • A higher-order system usually behaves like its slowest poles — the ones closest to the imaginary axis. The faster ones die out first.
  • Example: with poles at −5 and −1 ± 5j, the response looks almost exactly like the −1 ± 5j pair alone.
  • The exception: if a zero sits right next to the slow pole, it shrinks that residue and the next poles take over.

Specifications as regions in the plane

Settling time

Poles to the left of a vertical line: Re(p) more negative than some bound.

Overshoot

ζ above a minimum: the poles stay inside a wedge around the negative real axis.

Rise time

Poles far enough from the origin, or with a large enough imaginary part.

Together

A "good" region. Draw it on top of the root locus and pick a gain that lands the dominant poles inside it.

Steady-state error

  • From r to e is the sensitivity S = 1/(1 + L). For a step, ess = 1/(1 + L(0)): a bigger loop gain gives a smaller error.
  • If L has an integrator (a pole at 0) and the loop is stable, the step error is exactly zero, not just small.
  • The type of L is the number of poles at the origin. To follow a ramp of order q with zero error you need at least q + 1 integrators.

  • Disturbances work the same way. To fully reject a constant disturbance at the plant input, the integrator has to be in the controller.

P, I and D

P · proportional

More gain gives a smaller error and a faster response, but more noise. In second-order systems it also brings more oscillation.

I · integral

Removes the step error completely. Too much makes the response oscillate, and it can destabilize the loop.

D · derivative

Looks ahead at where the error is going: it adds damping and pulls the poles left. More noise, and in practice it needs a fast pole, s/(cs + 1).

PID

C(s) = (kDs² + kPs + kI)/s: two zeros and a pole at the origin. Place them, then compute kP, kI and kD.

The design recipe from the slides

  • Start with P and draw the root locus.
  • If the locus never passes through the good region, add a D term and redraw.
  • Pick the gain that puts the dominant poles in the good region.
  • If the steady-state error is still too big, add an I term and redraw.

The catch

  • Following the reference and rejecting disturbances needs S small. Keeping sensor noise out of the output needs T = L/(1 + L) small.
  • But S + T = 1, so both cannot be small at once. The way out is to make them small at different frequencies — which is where next week's frequency-domain tools come in.

If you keep one thing

Every time-domain spec is a fence in the complex plane. Settling time pushes the poles left, overshoot keeps them in a wedge, and integrators kill the steady-state error. P, I and D are how you bend the root locus into the region you want.
