Frequency response and Bode plots

✨AI-generated recap — check anything important against the slides

The root locus has its limits: it needs a finite set of poles and zeros, and it says little about uncertainty. This lecture starts on the other two classical tools. Both are built on one object you already know from week 5: G(jω), how a system stretches and delays a sinusoid.

Three classical tools

Root locus

Quick check of whether a design can work. Only for finite numbers of poles and zeros; hard to show uncertainty.

Nyquist plot

The most reliable closed-loop stability test, and it always applies. Harder to draw and to design with.

Bode plot

Easy to draw and easy to show uncertainty — the tool of choice for real design. Can mislead if the plant is unstable or non-minimum phase.

Frequency response, again

  • For a stable system, cos(ωt) in gives |G(jω)|·cos(ωt + ∠G(jω)) out in steady state: the same frequency, stretched and shifted.
  • G(jω) is a complex number for every ω. You can draw it in two ways: as a curve in the complex plane (the polar plot, leading to Nyquist), or as magnitude and phase against ω (the Bode plot).
  • You don't even need a model: excite a stable plant at a few frequencies, measure amplitude and phase, and you have its Bode plot.

How a Bode plot is drawn

  • Frequency on a log scale. Magnitude in decibels: 20·log10|G|. A factor of 10 is 20 dB, a factor of 2 is about 6 dB.
  • Phase in degrees, on a linear scale.
  • Because magnitudes multiply and phases add, on these axes the Bode plots of factors simply add up. Inverting G flips both plots about the horizontal axis.

The building blocks

Gain k

A flat line at 20·log10k. Phase 0°.

Integrator 1/s

Falls at −20 dB per decade through 0 dB at ω = 1. Phase −90° everywhere.

Stable pole 1/(τs + 1)

Flat at 0 dB, then −20 dB/decade after the corner at ω = 1/τ (−3 dB right at it). Phase from 0° to −90°, passing −45° at the corner.

Complex pair

Flat, then −40 dB/decade after ωn. Phase from 0° to −180°. Near ωn there is a peak of 1/(2ζ): the smaller ζ, the sharper the peak and the steeper the phase drop.

Zero τs + 1

The pole flipped: +20 dB/decade after the corner, phase up to +90°.

Non-minimum-phase zero −τs + 1

Same magnitude as a normal zero, but the phase goes down to −90°, like a pole. The magnitude alone cannot tell you it is there.

Putting it together

  • Step 1: rewrite G in Bode form, with every factor as (s/(−p) + 1) and the leftover constant out in front. For example, ½·(s + 2)(s + 10)/((s² + s + 1)(s + 5)) becomes 2·(s/2 + 1)(s/10 + 1)/((s² + s + 1)(s/5 + 1)).
  • Step 2: draw the straight-line approximation for each factor.
  • Step 3: add them. The low-frequency level is the Bode gain (here 2, about 6 dB). Each corner changes the slope by ±20 dB/decade for each pole or zero there.
  • An integrator in front tilts the whole thing: the plot starts at −20 dB/decade and −90°, as in the second example on the slides, 4·(s/4 + 1)/(s(s²/100 + s/10 + 1)).

Bode's law

  • For a system that is stable and minimum-phase, magnitude and phase are not independent.
  • If the magnitude slope is κ·20 dB/decade over about a decade or more, the phase there is roughly κ·90°. A −40 dB/decade stretch means about −180°.

The polar plot

  • The same G(jω), drawn as one curve in the complex plane, with ω running along it.
  • Sketch the Bode plot first, then read magnitude and angle off it to place the curve.
  • Only two things really matter: where it crosses the unit circle (|G| = 1) and where it crosses the negative real axis (phase −180°). That is the setup for Nyquist next week.

If you keep one thing

On a Bode plot every factor is a straight-line piece you can draw by hand, and the full plot is just their sum. Poles bend it down by 20 dB/decade and −90°, zeros bend it up — except a right-half-plane zero, which still pulls the phase down.
